About

A job board that attracts applicants: candidates browse vacancies and apply.

Challenge

Increase the conversion to applications without lowering the quality of filled-in profiles.
Refresh the UI.
Do it with minimal resources.

Result

+14% visitor-to-application conversion

Year over year, compared with the same period

  • Search
  • Returning visitors
  • Involvement blocks
  • Application form
  • UI

Search

Findings

24% of users left the site after seeing search results.
Some searches returned no results. The location field looked mandatory, so users didn’t see jobs in neighbouring cantons, even though some were ready to commute there.
The site isn’t a job aggregator and has fewer than 10k vacancies, so filtering too early cuts the results down.

Solution

We reworked the search field: location became an optional filter.
Users can also widen the search with a radius around the chosen cities (+30 km), so jobs in neighbouring cantons show up too.

After

Result

−26% searches with no results

Features for returning visitors

Findings

Returning users made up 38% of all traffic, but the site had nothing for this audience.

Solution

We added two features for returning visitors:

/ previous search/ viewed vacancies

Involvement blocks

Findings

The home page was the most common entry point, but 21% of users left right there.

Solution

Show users more useful information:

  • The latest vacancies, filtered by field and location
  • Vacancy statistics by city and industry

Application form

Findings

55% of visitors left this page without filling in the form.
To find a solution, we analysed how recruiters process applications, and found one more issue: recruiters often had to ask applicants for references and certificates separately, because they weren’t attached to the application.

Solution

Hide less important fields from the first step.
Split the uploads into document types in the attachment form.

In short

Design in Develux’s EdTech products was a service: two designers drew what product managers asked for, and few initiatives paid off. In three years I rebuilt it into a design and research function whose goals are tied to the success of the product teams.

How the process changed

Before

Discovery & Initiation

Research stayed shallow — PMs ran it on their own, while their attention went to the solution. Ideas reached the team already decided.

Discovery & Initiation

  • Product Manager

Delivery

The team had no say in the idea and no stake in its result. They shipped tasks — and nobody questioned the solution before it was built.

Delivery

  • UX/UI Design
  • Product Dev
  • Product Analytics

9%

Fewer than one in ten initiatives hit their success metric. Most of the effort went into things that didn’t move the business.

9%

Initiative success rate

After

Discovery

PMs no longer started from scratch. Researchers built a shared insight hub from surveys and interviews; with the PM, they turned it into an opportunity solution tree and first hypotheses.

Discovery

  • UX Research
  • Product Manager

Ideation & Filtering

Weak ideas were filtered out with research and product data before anyone built them. The PM owned the problem, the designer the solution, the researcher the insights, the analyst the data.

Ideation & Filtering

  • Product Manager
  • UX Research

The whole team could change the solution. Developers suggested cheaper builds with the same effect; analysts confirmed or killed hypotheses with product data.

Delivery

How I got there

  1. 01

    Led by example

    Worked hands-on with product managers to show a different approach: problem first, research, business metrics.

  2. 02

    Hired designers who research

    Once the approach proved itself, I hired designers who run user tests and surveys themselves.

  3. 03

    Bet on research, not on more designers

    When demand grew, I hired a UX researcher instead. It became a team of six with an insight hub that product managers start from.

  4. 04

    Built research into the process

    With the CPO and Ops, made it part of how product teams work, including ideation sessions based on insights.

  5. 05

    Added UX writing, rebuilt web design

    The content team became UX writing. The web team went from 7 people to 4, focused on speed and templates.

projects

Freelance marketplace for student writing

NDA
Role
Feature owner, product designer
Team
Product manager, analyst, UX researcher
Period of time
2025

In short

Choosing a writer was the biggest drop-off in the funnel, right before payment. Each order drew 30+ offers, and they all looked the same: perfect ratings, thousands of reviews, so customers couldn’t tell who to trust. Research showed what they actually look for, and I redesigned the offer card around it: a rating from recent orders, subject expertise, on-time delivery and writing samples.

  • +14%chose a writer
  • +6%first orders
  • +3.5%repeat orders

A/B test, all results statistically significant

Context

  1. 01A writer profile is rarely one person: usually it’s a small company, so it takes orders in any subject
  2. 02Orders are cheap, so writers take a lot of them, and every profile shows thousands of orders and reviews
  3. 03Choosing a writer is the last step before payment, so it drives the conversion of the whole funnel
  1. Post an order
  2. 30+ offers from writers
  3. Choose a writer
  4. Pay

Problem statement

Too many customers dropped off at the step where they choose a writer

Research & solution

Based on regular customer interviews, 7 user tests and 5 in-depth interviews

  1. Core problem

    All writers looked the same, and nothing showed who fits this particular order

01All writers looked the same

  1. Insight

    Every writer had a huge number of reviews

    Decision

    A rating based on the last 100 orders

  2. Insight

    Every writer had a high total order count, and it looked inflated

    Decision

    The total order count was removed

  3. Insight

    Greeting messages were templates and didn’t help

    Decision

    Collapsed into a message button with a counter

02Nothing showed who fits this order

  1. Insight

    No sign of how deep a writer’s expertise is in a given subject

    Decision

    Highlights for each subject, based on the writer’s past work

  2. Insight

    Orders are placed close to the deadline, and customers need time to edit the text themselves

    Decision

    An on-time metric: how often orders are delivered on time

  3. Insight

    Customers worried the writing would look too professional for them, or not professional enough

    Decision

    Writers can add samples for each subject

Results

Measured in an A/B test; every result reached statistical significance

  • Chose a writer+14%
  • First orders+6%
  • Repeat orders+3.5%

Why +14% choices didn’t become +14% orders

After choosing a writer, many customers were surprised that they had to pay right away. Paying upfront is part of the business model, so some of the extra choices still dropped off at the payment step.
